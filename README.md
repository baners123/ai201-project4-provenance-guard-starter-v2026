# Provenance Guard

## What This Does

Provenance Guard is a local backend service for a writing platform. A client sends `POST /submit` with `text` and `creator_id`. The service validates the request, assigns a `content_id`, runs two independent signals, combines their scores, maps the combined score to one of three reader-facing labels, writes the decision to `logs/audit.jsonl`, and returns structured JSON.

The first signal comes from `detector.py` and measures how predictable the text is to a local language model. The second comes from `stylometry.py` and measures sentence-length variation, vocabulary variety, and punctuation density. `scoring.py::combine_signals` combines the scores, and `scoring.py::score_to_label` maps the final score to high-confidence human, unsure, or high-confidence AI.

A writer can challenge a result with `POST /appeal`. The service checks that the `content_id` exists, records the writer's reasoning, changes the status to `under_review`, and confirms the appeal.

## Signals and Scoring

### Signal One: Model Signal

`detector.py::model_signal` measures text predictability with a local GPT-2 model. Lower perplexity becomes a higher AI-likelihood score.

Its blind spot is that predictability is not authorship. Plain human writing, second-language writing, and short conventional sentences can also be predictable.

### Signal Two: Style Signal

`stylometry.py::style_signal` combines sentence-length spread, type-token ratio, and punctuation density.

Its blind spot is that it cannot understand meaning or authorship. Punctuation changes, typos, sentence splitting, and text length can change the score without changing who wrote the text.

### Combining Rule

The rule is in `scoring.py::combine_signals`.

```text
combined score = 0.65(model score) + 0.35(style score)
```

The model signal receives more weight because it directly measures predictability. The style signal receives less weight because surface formatting is easier to manipulate.

The original Unit 7 thresholds were:

```text
0.00 to 0.30: high-confidence human
above 0.30 and below 0.75: unsure
0.75 to 1.00: high-confidence AI
```

I intentionally used a wide unsure range because falsely accusing a human writer is more costly than allowing some AI-generated writing to remain uncertain.

### Real Signal Disagreement

One real submission produced:

```text
model score: 0.6459
style score: 0.2639
combined score: 0.5122
```

The two signals disagreed substantially. The model found the wording relatively predictable, while the style measurements looked more human. The combined result landed in the unsure band, which is the behavior I wanted when the evidence is mixed.

## Label Variants

The original Unit 7 label ranges were:

| Label | Score range | Exact reader-facing text |
|---|---|---|
| High-confidence human | 0.00 to 0.30 | We think this was probably written by a person. |
| Unsure | Above 0.30 and below 0.75 | We are not confident enough to say whether this was written by a person or generated by AI. No action should be taken based on this result alone. |
| High-confidence AI | 0.75 to 1.00 | This text shows strong signs of AI generation, but this result is not proof of authorship. The writer can appeal this decision for review. |

## How I Used AI

### Moment 1

**What I asked:** I used AI to review how sentence variation, vocabulary variety, and punctuation density could be converted into one bounded stylometry score.

**What came back:** The measurements use different numeric ranges and do not all point in the same direction.

**What I changed:** I normalized the measurements before combining them, inverted the human-style result so higher always means more AI-like, and documented that surface formatting can manipulate the signal.

### Moment 2

**What I asked:** I used AI to review the submission, validation, logging, and appeal flow for edge cases.

**What came back:** Important cases included missing or empty text, invalid `creator_id`, very large text, unknown appeal IDs, and detector failures.

**What I changed:** I added explicit validation, 4xx JSON errors for bad input, rejection logging, a text-size limit, and an appeal check that requires an existing `content_id`.

# Unit 8

## Rate Limiting

The service limits a creator to 8 submissions per minute and 100 submissions per day.

I chose 8 submissions per minute because it allows a real writer to retry or submit several drafts while still stopping a script that rapidly probes the detector.

I tested the limiter with:

```bash
python run_attacks.py --flood 15
```

The result was:

```text
200 200 200 200 200 200 200 200 429 429 429 429 429 429 429
```

Eight requests were accepted and seven were rate limited. The rejected requests were also recorded in the audit log with `reason: rate_limited` and `status: rejected`.

## Run Log and Verdicts

I ran each of my five acceptance criteria three times before making an improvement.

| Criterion | Target | Run 1 | Run 2 | Run 3 | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. False positives on my own writing | At most 1 of 10 labeled high-confidence AI | 0/10 | 0/10 | 0/10 | MET |
| 2. AI and human scores are separated | AI average at least 0.20 above human average | 0.2301 gap | 0.2301 gap | 0.2301 gap | MET |
| 3. All three labels are reachable | Human, unsure, and AI all appear | 2/3 | 2/3 | 2/3 | MISSED |
| 4. Valid appeals move to review and are logged | 5/5 | 5/5 | 5/5 | 5/5 | MET |
| 5. Bad input fails safely | 4/4 safe 4xx responses and logged | 4/4 | 4/4 | 4/4 | MET |

For Criterion 2, the AI calibration group averaged `0.6412`, while the human group averaged `0.4111`. The difference was `0.2301`, which met the target.

### Criterion 3 Diagnosis

Criterion 3 was MISSED in all three trials.

**Stage:** Label mapping

**Mechanism:** The high-confidence AI threshold was `0.75`, but the highest combined score among the six calibration inputs was only `0.6497`. The two AI calibration samples scored `0.6004` and `0.6497`, so both remained in the unsure range.

The six calibration results from the first run were:

```text
human   0.2290
human   0.2678
unsure  0.6004
unsure  0.6497
human   0.2302
unsure  0.4249
```

The human and borderline samples remained considerably lower, but the original AI threshold was too high for any calibration input to reach the high-confidence AI label.

## The Improvement

I changed the high-confidence AI threshold from `0.75` to `0.62`.

The before evaluation showed that Criterion 3 failed in all three trials because none of the calibration inputs reached the AI label. The strongest AI calibration sample scored `0.6497`, while the other AI sample scored `0.6004`.

I chose `0.62` because it is a limited change to the label mapping instead of changing the detector, stylometry signal, or combining rule.

With the new threshold:

```text
0.6004 remains unsure
0.6497 becomes high-confidence AI
0.4249 remains unsure
0.2678 remains high-confidence human
0.2302 remains high-confidence human
0.2290 remains high-confidence human
```

This should allow all three labels to become reachable while preserving an unsure range for borderline results.

I did not change either signal or the `65%` model and `35%` stylometry weighting.

The main tradeoff is that lowering the AI threshold could increase false positives. Because false accusations are the most costly error in this system, I need to rerun all five acceptance criteria after the threshold change and confirm that Criterion 1 continues to protect human writing.

## What's Still Broken

This section should be completed after the official attack set and the after evaluation are run.

The main remaining risk is that the detector can be manipulated through surface-level changes such as punctuation, typos, and sentence restructuring. These changes can affect the stylometry signal without changing the underlying authorship.

If this system were used in production, I would favor fewer false accusations against human writers even if more AI-written text remained in the unsure range. A false accusation directly affects a writer, while an unsure result can be sent for additional review.
